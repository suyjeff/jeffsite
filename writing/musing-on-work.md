---
title: "Musing on Work"
---

There's something about *doing* that has become foggy in my first few years in the workforce. In university, you learn through active listening, engaged problem solving, and endless assessments. The common thread is that these activities are always *assigned*. Someone with authority, be it an experienced professor or a seasoned teaching assistant, is trusted to know what the next best thing is for you to do. Follow this path and by the end of a semester, there's a pretty decent chance you'll become, at the very least, sufficient at Matrix Algebra or Fluid Dynamics. Under the careful watch of your advisors, professors, and classmates, a few years will pass and suddenly you're a reasonably capable mathematician or scientist.

This structure exists professionally, but without the same guardrails. Fail a course in university and your academic advisor will appear right around the corner with stern guidance. Their warning: "how will you be prepared for the real world"? But what happens when you're in the real world? Sure, guardrails also exist in corporations to ensure employees are growing and performing adequately. But these are largely informally measured. They won't shy away from termination if that investment required to get an employee from sufficient to proficient is greater than that of simply hiring a new, more proficient employee.

There is an expectation during this process that you are driving *something*. Gone are the well-paced assignments and readings that guide you towards a complete understanding of a subject. Now, it is you who must create this understanding. 

When you need something done, you needn't triple check the instructions and follow the rubric. You simply do it. You might half-ass the thing, you might spend way too long noodling on the details, you might be forced to drop it if another priority emerges. The common thread? You take the reigns.

It is still a wild thought to me that in some cases, the best thing I can do next is something that my manager or teammates haven't yet thought of. We are on our own, but we are also working together with a shared goal and corporate-speak "OKRs" and "KPIs" to guide us. Within this new structure, it may not be up to you to determine the end goal or the timeline, but it is up to you to *do something*. 

See, what you've gained in the past few years after university is (hopefully) a loose understanding of what is correct. You've machined your instincts through tons of repetitions and through simply being a part of processes that did and did not work. You know within a smaller project-level scope how to compare a good and bad outcome. Now, it's up to you to apply this to a slightly larger vision: your team, your work unit.

Think logically: if you know the goals of your team, if you know the talents that you possess, if you know through instinct and through past experience what the next steps might be, should you not be able to formulate a set of tasks that will make progress? The challenge is communication and collaboration. I may know what to do next, but can I convince the others on my team that these steps are valuable? Can I get them to join or support me along the way?

The only way to garner this support is to be skilled in advocacy and prove your judgement. Your team must look upon you and your body of work and think: "Hm, that seems like a good decision in isolation, an even better one with his expertise and ability behind it, and an even better one because I think he can accomplish it."

This is not a guarantee. You might have an intuition that is incorrect or infeasible. Thankfully, you have the judgement of those around you–who you either trust or want to trust–to help steer you properly. So talk, collaborate, find like-minded people or those who support you. Prove yourself as an individual who is capable of determining what is next. No longer do you have the safe haven of a tuition and grade report guiding your tasks. You exist in a free world where you chose to join a team to accomplish a set of goals. Accomplish them, and take the agency that has been granted to you (or, that you have been burdened with) and move forward. Do not wait, do not twiddle your thumbs, and do not exist in a dream-like state of fear about the pitfalls of your own decisions. When you face them, you will face them.
