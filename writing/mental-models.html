Mental ModelsBack

March 3, 2026

Design can often feel like a journey through the abstract. On its surface, the many choices that amount to a design are fairly simple. By taking already expertly-defined source elements like typefaces (with their incredible depth of weights, sizes, and features), auto-adjusting divs, interface-ready color standards, and the foundations of layouts, it is not (and has never been) difficult to create something usable.

Continuing the exploration into building blocks yields similar simple definitions and guidelines. Operating systems have already blazed a path of understanding which accommodates both novice users, attempting to create a simple document or open an email account, and the most niche use cases, such as tinkering with the source elements and functionality.

The usage patterns of experts and novices alike can always be broken down into extremely simple actions. Complex and highly personal workflows are nothing more than thousands of movements in a file system, hundreds of miles of scrolling and cursor movement, carefully coordinated key taps, and basic literacy. Wire these base actions together in their endless permutations, and you can accomplish virtually anything with any interface.

If this is so conceptually simple, what are the qualities and considerations that create the stratification which allows for the most beautiful and effective designs to be clearly better than their contemporaries? Why are some applications, however simple in their purpose and structure, so much more usable than others? It cannot simply be that the designers of these applications are using more niche visual techniques or prettier animations–all of these things quickly diffuse across the design world (e.g. Linear's landing page, iOS' mastery of basic applications, graphical filesystems). This is not to say that some designers and teams do not have better craft than others. But in the aggregate, it is unlikely that these somewhat subjective measures of taste are the main differentiator. In the vast sea of aesthetics, none of them truly "win" (as a thought exercise to prove the inadequacy of craft alone, ask yourself if the timing of various design trends had anything to do with effectiveness).

To me, beautiful and effective design emerges when the designer has a deep understanding of the mental models of what they are creating. This is vague, but necessarily so. To conceptualize this, imagine you are in an imaginary dream space where everything is white: the floor, the ceiling, the subliminal–similar to the worlds that often exist in between life and death in movies (e.g. when Harry Potter talks to Dumbledore in his retreat from death). In this dream space, the only thing that exists aside from your perception is the thing you are designing. You can manipulate this object in three-dimensional space, meaning you can spin it around, flip it upside-down, take it apart, and freely use it as defined by the rules of its greater system. In this world, everything in design becomes an object.

Effective design is the deep exploration of the function and presentation of that object relative to human expectation. I refer to this as the "mental model" of an object (smarter minds might say "affordances"). The entire goal of design is to ensure that every object's mental model makes sense in itself and relative to the objects around it. There is no limit to the size and scope of an object that you can observe in this whitespace. Take a simple form as an example: each input's text and frame are separate objects but also can form a new object, the stacking of inputs into a cohesive form is also an object, the various states possible of a the submit button form an object, and on and on.

Everything you create must function in accordance with the "mental model". You can boil this down to well-known affordances: a push door handle should not look the same as a pull door handle, a switch should look different when it's on versus when it's off, you cannot read a book if you cannot tell how to progress to the next page. These are fairly basic considerations, but they are fundamentally the only truth that exists in design. They are probabilistic and heavily contextual in nature, but they are all functionally human. Consider the differences in greeting customs that exist between the Americas, Asia, Europe, and tribes in far-off lands. Some greet with waves and handshakes, some greet by bowing their torso, some read the angle with which you bow as a message in itself. Still, they are all greetings. Design is a deep exploration of how to build things in alignment with our basic humanity.

Nowadays, especially in the software world of two-dimensional interaction, we have strayed fairly far from everything having an obvious connection to our source wiring. It is less easy to, say, find the least common denominator between a complex logs monitoring flow and our basic primal expectations. Thankfully, humans have also developed the context to allow for digital interactions to be worked-in to our source code. Any flow can be deconstructed into basic actions that have an objective mental model in all humans. Language barriers aside, I am confident you could explain all concepts of a password field (from the text and input to the show and hide states) to any living being. Even the most remote hunter-gatherers can understand the concept of wanting to keep a piece of information private. What are the means that one might go through to achieve this? In the luxury of our digital worlds, that might be masking the password in generic dots or abstracting the password away into a unique biological definition. In the prehistoric world, this might manifest in the form of using large leaves or a big boulder to protect your food. Or in our most primal states, hiding something behind your back.

When designing interfaces, in any dimension, you can always manipulate your objects in the white void to determine correctness. Everything does not have an objectively correct function, but everything does have a set of values which are expected by our basic humanity. Find this set of values and play with it. You'll find it's quite vast even in its limited scope. There are thousands of different ways to define our natural world and behaviors, yet all of them are decidedly human.
